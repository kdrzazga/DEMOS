## PETSCII: How a 1970s Character Set Became a Living Art Form

PETSCII is the character set baked into Commodore's 8-bit machines
the PET, VIC-20, C-64, Plus/4, and C-16.



On the Commodore 64 specifically,
those symbols got a thicker stroke weight, likely to stay
legible on a television screen. This is the character set
most people associate with the term "PETSCII," and it's
the one this study focuses on.

What makes PETSCII interesting isn't just the symbols themselves but the constraints they impose. Every character occupies a fixed cell in a 40-by-25 grid, drawn from a palette of just 16 colors, at an effective resolution of 320 by 200 pixels. Nothing can be resized, curved smoothly, or placed off-grid. That rigidity is presented as a feature, not a bug — a "text mosaic" tradition, connected in spirit to older grid-based crafts like mosaics, weaving, and quilting, where a fixed unit of composition becomes the whole basis for expression. The tight boundaries function as both a limitation and a creative engine.

**A brief history of the format.** PETSCII drawing was possible right from the BASIC prompt, letting people mock up images without writing a single line of code — an accessible, almost toy-like entry point to computer graphics for a generation of hobbyists. Early Commodore 64 games leaned on it heavily before the industry matured and shifted toward smoother bitmap and sprite graphics, at which point character art started reading as amateurish, "type-in" software rather than serious game production. It found a second home on bulletin board systems through the 1980s and 90s, used for menus, logos, and even crude animations, before BBSes were largely abandoned for the internet in the late 1990s and PETSCII activity quieted down to a handful of dedicated enthusiasts.

The format's real resurgence began in the early 2010s. An artist working under the name Max Capacity started producing PETSCII-style work using modern cross-platform tools rather than actual Commodore hardware, which let him work at higher resolutions, use more colors, and build longer animations than a 64-kilobyte machine could ever hold — while still keeping the classic aesthetic. Around the same time, an online Plain PETSCII Competition in 2013 reignited interest specifically within the demoscene (the community built around technically impressive, competition-driven creative coding), spurring a wave of new authoring tools and pushing the format's technical ceiling higher than it had ever been.

**Tools, old and new.** The researchers surveyed fourteen different PETSCII editors, split roughly into old native programs that run on actual Commodore hardware and newer cross-platform tools built for modern computers. The native tools — things like Digital Paint, Kaleidoscope, and Tyronpaint from the late 80s and 90s, or the more actively maintained PETSCII Editor by fieserWolf, updated as recently as 2017 — are keyboard-only, since mice never caught on for Commodore machines, and generally default to a black background suited to BBS-style graphics. Modern cross-platform tools bring in things that would've been impractical on original hardware: mouse input, generous screen space, large undo buffers, and animation support. Some, like JP LeBreton's Playscii and its predecessor EDSCII, go further and let artists do things the original machines never allowed at all — independently coloring a character's foreground and background, or swapping out the palette entirely. That flexibility is a double-edged sword: it makes the creative process easier, but it also turns "PETSCII" from a strict technical format into more of a loose visual style that can be imitated without the underlying constraints.

Interestingly, some serious artists deliberately avoid these easier modern tools, preferring the discipline (and, the authors suggest, near-ceremonial feel) of typing directly on original Commodore hardware, treating faithfulness to the vintage keyboard and display as part of the art's meaning.

The authors didn't just study existing tools from the outside — they built their own PETSCII editor in the Processing programming environment starting in 2013, and used four years of hands-on development and daily use as a research method in itself. Building the tool surfaced insights that pure observation wouldn't have: for instance, that the default character layout on Commodore keyboards is fairly unintuitive, and that regrouping visually related symbols (lines near other lines, corners near other corners) makes the whole system far easier to navigate. They also noticed that many shapes are mirror images of each other hidden across the character set, which led them to build flexible selection, rotation, and flipping tools that exploit that symmetry — features that have since become fairly standard across other modern editors too. Another recurring lesson: separating the "what" (which characters) from the "how" (which colors) tends to produce better workflows, so most tools let artists sketch shapes first and recolor afterward, sometimes with dedicated recoloring or "magic lens" style tools.

**Style categories.** Drawing on a sample of thirty works pulled from five recent PETSCII competitions plus five additional standout pieces, the authors identify three dominant visual approaches:

- **Line drawing** — built from box-drawing and line characters, closest in spirit to classic ASCII art, often evoking 80s nostalgia through the iconic blue BASIC background. Common in menus, games, and especially small "directory art" pieces, but seen as a somewhat naive style within the more competitive demoscene.
- **Text mosaic** — built from solid geometric blocks (squares, triangles, circles) rather than lines, favoring bold, colorful, textural patterns where the individual character shapes stay legible.
- **Pixel style** — the most technically demanding approach, where artists exploit sub-character detail, using ordinary characters as improvised "pixels" and blending them across boundaries so it becomes genuinely hard to tell where one character cell ends and the next begins.

Many strong pieces combine all three techniques in a single image — using mosaic blocks for a body, line work for outlines, and pixel-level tricks for fine detail like eyes or facial features. This blending underlies the more recent trend toward photorealism in PETSCII, where artists chase photographic likeness using density-graded characters to fake shading gradients (running from dense, "heavy" characters down to sparse ones like periods or commas), plus tricks borrowed from bitmap art — hand-tuned anti-aliasing through careful color choices, or dithering effects using checkerboard characters, though the coarse 4-by-4 checkerboard available on the C-64 makes this a fairly blunt tool by comparison to finer options historically available on other platforms.

Popular culture shows up constantly as subject matter — Darth Vader, Pikachu, characters from specific video games, internet memes — which the authors note keeps PETSCII tethered to broader visual culture even as it operates inside such a narrow technical box.

**Why this format persists.** The concluding argument is that PETSCII's appeal lies precisely in its shared, fixed constraints. Because everyone is working within the exact same grid, palette, and character set, competitions become a meaningful measure of skill — comparisons are fair in a way they wouldn't be with an open-ended medium. It's also comparatively low-effort to get started with (easier than composing chip music or building a full demo), which keeps drawing in newcomers even as it also floods competitions with less polished beginner work.

The authors also observe a split in how the format relates to nostalgia. Line drawings on that classic blue background lean hard into evoking the 1980s. Photorealistic pieces do the opposite — pushing so far from the traditional blocky look that viewers sometimes can't even tell the image is built from characters at all, meaning the format's constraints get hidden behind sheer craftsmanship rather than celebrated outright. Both impulses — comfortable nostalgia and virtuosic denial of the medium's limits — coexist within the same small, decades-old symbol set, which is really the paper's central point: a rigid, 1970s-era technical constraint can still function today as an open, evolving creative platform rather than a museum piece.
