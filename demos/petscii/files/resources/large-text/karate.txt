Karate, the noble duel
If someone asked me what genre I enjoy most when it comes to retro gaming,
I would answer without hesitation: karate games, but only those released between 1985 and 1988.
At that time, there was a very interesting competition – two companies released almost identical
series at the same time. System 3 created the International Karate series, while Beam Software,
 created three Exploding Fist games, released by Melbourne House and later Firebird.
Why am I mentioning them? Well, because I always feel like playing them.

I've written quite a lot about how much fun it is to fight,
but I haven't mentioned any specific titles yet. In the beginning,
I recommend trying International Karate, which graphically is a marvel considering it was released in 1985
(Commodore). The karatekas move very naturally (could this be a motion capture technology?).
We fight in several capital cities of the world, admiring characteristic landscapes in the background,
while nearby, the referee makes sure that the guys don't start kicking balls of each other.
Each competitor can score half a point or a full point, depending on the cleanliness of the punch.
In practice, this means that each strike has its optimal distance from the target.
Just like in real life, to kick someone in the stomach, you must stand further away than
when punching someone in his solar plexus. The point of keeping the right distance is
essential to winning a fight. The combat ends when we score two full points or when the time runs out.
Almost all strikes, except low kicks and undercuts, can be blocked by simply stepping back.
Here, we can quote the well-known saying, “cut your coat according to your cloth”.
What does this mean specifically? Well, people show great creativity when they deliberately
avoid the limitations that were imposed on them. When these games were created nearly forty years ago,
there were no controllers with a dozen buttons, analog sticks, and other bells and whistles.
Instead, there was a joystick - one directional stick with a single button.
It is for this reason that such a brilliantly simple and effective system was invented – till this day,
I consider this type of blocking to be the best possible.
After three wins, you are promoted to a higher rank and our belt color changes,
 which is directly related to greater skill of our opponents. After each round,
 there are two alternating interludes. The first involves breaking bricks with your head (ouch!).
 Waggle your joystick right-left, right-left as quickly as possible.
 In the second case, we need to avoid various objects flying in our direction.

Another competing title is Way of the Exploding Fist, released a year earlier by Melbourne House/Beam Software. Here, we don't earn belts but rather advance straight on to the next level. After every few fights, the scenery changes, and in the background, just like in the competition, an old man sitting on the ground acts as a referee. The game is generally very similar, but unfortunately, it falls short in detail.  Music, although not distracting, does not stay in your head as long as the chiptune from International Karate. The animation is clearly slower and less fluent, and the quality of background is poor. Nevertheless, it is worth giving this game a chance, if only to notice the nuances that differentiate between these titles.

This game can be described as a karate adventure game. At that time, there was a clear division in fighting games between “tournament” games, in which you fought a single opponent in a knock-out system with an emphasis on a variety of punches, and “walking” fighting games, in which a tough guy, similar to Chuck Norris, travelled one direction and beat dozens of enemies – a beat’em up game of that era.
Elements of adventure game with one-on-one combat and a variety of punches were nicely combined in Fist II. This turned out to be a bull’s-eye. As mentioned earlier, once you figured out the mechanics of the previous games, they were best enjoyed when playing with another person. However, Fist II is a treat for a single player.
